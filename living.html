A guided overview

Recognize the time. Then create it.

In his lecture on “The Selection of the Time,” Ikeda Sensei explores why Nichiren Daishonin saw his own troubled era as the time to spread Nam-myoho-renge-kyo. The lecture connects this history with the mission of kosen-rufu and the pursuit of a peaceful, compassionate society.

Rather than allowing conflict or confusion to determine our direction, Sensei emphasizes the importance of courage, solidarity, and compassionate action.

The right time is created through determination

“The ‘time’ is not simply an objective condition”Ikeda Sensei · Living Buddhism, November 2026, p. 37

Sensei recalls his own sustained daimoku and actions for kosen-rufu, and his commitment to foster others. The principle is relevant beyond the calendar: meaningful change is not just something that happens to us. It is something we work to bring about.

When applying this lecture to the remaining weeks of 2026, it is helpful to distinguish our own personal goals from the lecture’s central emphasis on advancing peace and kosen-rufu through compassionate action.

A compassionate society of respect for all

The lecture describes human revolution and the courage to help others as fundamental to creating peace. Rather than seeing victory only as an individual achievement, the message encourages us to treasure each person.

Reflection: What one action can I take today that advances both my own human revolution and the happiness of another person?

Nam-myoho-renge-kyo Will Spread Widely in the Latter Day

Question: Is there no way to determine them [i.e., the time and the capacity of the people]?

Answer: Let us borrow the eye of the Buddha to consider this question of time and capacity. Let us use the sun of the Buddha to illuminate the nation. ...

There is no doubt that our present age corresponds to the fifth five-hundred-year period described in the Great Collection Sutra, when “the pure Law will become obscured and lost.” But after the pure Law is obscured and lost, the great pure Law of Nam-myoho-renge-kyo, the heart and core of the Lotus Sutra, will surely spread and be widely declared throughout the land of Jambudvipa [this world in which we dwell]—with its eighty thousand kingdoms, their eighty thousand rulers, and the ministers and people in the domain of each ruler—just as the name of Amida is now chanted by the mouths of the priests, nuns, laymen, and laywomen in Japan. (The Writings of Nichiren Daishonin, vol. 1, pp. 540–41)

Nam-myoho-renge-kyo Will Spread Widely in the Latter Day

(“The Selection of the Time,” The Writings of Nichiren Daishonin, vol. 1, pp. 540— 41) If it is true, then, that the Buddha expounded the Law not according to people’s capacity but according to the time, the following question arises: “When is the time for preaching the Hinayana or provisional Mahayana sutras, and when is the time for preaching the Lotus Sutra?” (WND-1, 540). How can ordinary people know the time and capacity with regard to the Buddha’s various teachings? By way of response, Nichiren Daishonin says: “Let us borrow the eye of

the Buddha to consider this question of time and capacity. Let us use the sun of the Buddha to illuminate the nation” (WND-1, 540). The “eye of the Buddha” means the Buddha’s insightful vision that can perceive the true aspect of all phenomena. The “sun of the Buddha” means the Buddha wisdom that, like the sun’s rays, can illuminate everything. To “borrow the eye of the Buddha to consider this question of time and capacity,” therefore, means to determine the time and capacity in light of the sutras, while to “use the sun of the Buddha to illuminate the nation” means to understand the reality of the land in light of the sutras. Here, Nichiren underscores the importance of judging matters of time and capacity as well as the situ-

ation in the land, not based on the arbitrary opinions of different Buddhist teachers or scholars, but on the Buddha’s own teachings.

As a key sutra reference for determining the time, he cites the section in the Great Collection Sutra where Shakyamuni predicts the future after his passing in terms of “five fivehundred-year periods.”4 These five periods are respectively known as 1) the age of attaining liberation, 2) the age of meditation, 3) the age of reading, reciting and listening, 4) the age of building temples and stupas and 5) the age of conflicts, which is also described more specifically as “an age of quarrels and disputes when the pure Law becomes obscured and lost.” Nichiren then proceeds to give a detailed description of which teachings of the Buddha flourish and spread during each of these fivehundred-year periods (see WND-1, 544-50).

It is the fifth five-hundred-year period, however, that is Nichiren Daishonin’s main focus. As I have noted already, this age of conflict is a time when quarrels and disputes arise in the realm of Buddhism and

the pure Law, the correct teaching of the Buddha, becomes obscured and lost (see WND-1, 541). This, in fact, perfectly describes the situation in Japan during the Daishonin’s day.

In Japan, Shakyamuni’s Buddhism had split into what were known as the eight or ten schools,5 with each asserting its correctness in opposition to the others; the Buddha’s teachings became increasingly confused and jumbled. The lack of a sound philosophy or belief system caused the people’s lives to become disordered. In addition, there had been a series of natural disasters and instances of civil strife, which inflicted much misery and suffering on people throughout the land.

The Pure Land (Nembutsu) school of the day taught that—after the pure Law of Shakyamuni Buddha’s other teachings had been obscured and lost—the great pure Law expounded in the three Pure Land sutras6 would spread (see WND-1, 541). This arose from a view that placed disproportionate emphasis on people’s capacity, insisting that the easy-to-practice way of the Nembutsu was suited to the people of the Latter Day, given

their inferior capacity.

Nichiren, however, places foremost importance on the time, a stance that is based on his in-depth study of the sutras. He therefore clearly states in this writing that after Shakyamuni’s pure Law has been obscured and lost, it will be time for “the great pure Law of Nam-myohorenge-kyo, the heart and core of the Lotus Sutra” to spread widely throughout the world (see WND-1, 541). This is Nichiren’s towering declaration as a person actively committed to transforming the Latter Day from an age of Buddhism in decline into an age of kosen-rufu and the flourishing of the correct teaching of the Mystic Law in the Latter Day.

The teaching to be spread in the Latter Day to free people from suffering is the great Law of Nichiren Buddhism of sowing7—that is, Nammyoho-renge-kyo—which has the power to directly activate the Buddha nature in each individual's life, irrespective of their capacity for understanding. As the fundamental “seed of Buddhahood” by which all Buddhas are born, the Law of Nam-

myoho-renge-kyo enables people of this defiled latter age to positively transform their lives on the most fundamental level.

The Lotus Sutra’s Prediction of the Spread of the Mystic Law in the Latter Day

During the fifth five-hundred-year period, eminent priests who are possessed by evil demons will be found everywhere throughout the country. At that time, a single wise man will appear. The eminent priests who are possessed by evil demons will deceive the ruler, his ministers, and the common people into slandering and abusing this man, attacking him with sticks, staves, shards, and rubble, and condemning him to exile or death. Prior to this section, Nichiren Daishonin cites a number of passages from the Lotus Sutra, including from “Medicine King” and “Encouraging Devotion,” the 23rd and 13th chapters,10 to corroborate his assertion that the Mystic Law will spread widely in the Latter Day (see WND-1, 542). In this present section, he proceeds to summarize these passages into essentially the following five main points:

1. During the fifth five-hundred-year period— the “age of quarrels and disputes,” when the teachings of Shakyamuni Buddha become obscured and lost—the land will be filled with eminent Buddhist priests, who are “possessed by evil demons.” 11 This is a reference to the three powerful enemies,12 who the “Encouraging Devotion” chapter predicts will appear in the evil age after the Buddha’s passing, the most formidable of which is the group known as “arrogant false sages.”

2. At that time a “single wise man” will appear. This, needless to say, refers to Nichiren Daishonin himself. 3. Eminent priests possessed by demons will slander and defame this wise person to the secular authorities and influential people of the day, inciting them to physically attack him “with sticks, staves, shards, and rubble” and to condemn him to exile or death. 4. When this wise person is persecuted by the ruler and people throughout the land, strange occurrences and unusual disturbances will appear in the heavens and on earth as a form of rebuke from the heavenly deities. If this warning is not heeded, then “great struggles and disputes such as have never been known in the past” will break out in the world.

5. At that time, fearing the destruction of their nation or the loss of their lives, the ruler and common people will put their faith in this wise person whom they had earlier persecuted, and come to chant Nammyoho-renge-kyo.

In terms of Nichiren’s time in 13thcentury Japan, “great struggles and

disputes such as have never been known in the past” indicated invasion by the Mongol forces—an attack by a foreign power, something Japan had never previously experienced. As I mentioned earlier, in “On Establishing the Correct Teaching for the Peace of the Land,” written more than a decade before “The Selection of the Time,” Nichiren had warned that this disaster of foreign invasion would befall Japan [as a result of the entire nation turning against the correct teaching of Buddhism]. He had submitted this treatise to remonstrate with the country’s rulers, knowingly risking persecution in order to save people from the tragedy and suffering of war.

I once published a volume of lectures on “The Selection of the Time” (in 1964). Mr. Toda had made an initial start on writing a series of lectures on this work, and I brought the project to completion after his death; the finished volume, therefore, was a joint production of mentor and disciple.

In this early series, I remember discussing at length the question of

how Nichiren’s reference to “great struggles and disputes such as have never been known in the past” should be interpreted in the present age. I noted that some might take the view that this vision of unprecedented war and strife engulfing the world alludes to the future outbreak of a Third World War. But any such global conflict in our nuclear age, I observed, would mean almost certain destruction for the human race; given this scenario, therefore, we must absolutely ensure that a Third World War never happens. Based on this reasoning, I explained that I chose to interpret “great struggles and disputes such as have never been known in the past” as referring to the Second World War, and to continue emphasizing the importance of courageously pressing ahead on the path of kosen-rufu to create a world where peace prevails.

My sentiments today are the same. Rather, they have only grown stronger. We absolutely have to achieve peace. Last year, I issued a nuclear disarmament proposal.13 In Mr. Toda’s landmark declaration for the abolition of nuclear weapons (in September 1957), he passionately

stated: “It is my wish to attack the problem at its root, that is, to rip out the claws that are hidden in the very depths of this issue.” Making his spirit my own, I have taken action toward the goal of realizing a world without nuclear weapons. I would now like to pass on the baton of worldwide kosen-rufu— that is, the baton of peace creation—to the youth of Soka.

For all people to live in peace and security is without doubt the central focus of Nichiren Buddhism. In “On Establishing the Correct Teaching for the Peace of the Land,” he explains that the reason he so strongly rebukes the ruler is to prevent the turmoil of war that would arise from the disasters of internal strife and foreign invasion—the only remaining two of the seven disasters14 predicted in the sutras that had not yet appeared in the Japan of the Daishonin’s day.

“The Selection of the Time” can also be viewed as an ardent call for peace before it is too late. “Let us prevent the occurrence of great strife and conflict that causes people suffering! Let us realize a peaceful world for the

prosperity and security of people everywhere!”—this is our fundamental stance as Buddhists and the spirit of Nichiren Buddhism. Constructing a Compassionate Society of Respect for All Nichiren Daishonin appeared in the “age of quarrels and disputes” of the Latter Day of the Law. More specifically, it was the Kamakura period (1192-1333), which saw the first ever establishment of a military regime in Japan. It was also a time when the country faced the threat of foreign invasion. Taking these circumstances into account, an apt subtitle for “The Selection of the Time” might be “Choosing Peace.”

And, as I stated at the outset, the Soka Gakkai emerged in the period between World Wars I and IL. This was a time when Japan was rushing headlong down the path to militarism. It is also a fact that the SGI was inaugurated during a period when the Cold War continued to drag on and tensions were running high between China and the Soviet Union. What should humanity learn from

these two world wars of the 20th century? That the absolute truths that “nothing is more tragic and cruel than war” and “nothing is more precious than peace” need to become universal human values. Hence, the profound significance of our efforts to spread the life-affirming principles and ideals of Nichiren Buddhism toward the realization of lasting peace.

The “claws that are hidden in the very depths of the issue [of nuclear weapons],” of which Mr. Toda spoke in his declaration, in fact, refer to the fundamental darkness or ignorance15 that resides in the depths of human life. This innate darkness gives rise to deluded impulses that manifest as greed, anger and foolishness and are the root source of all evil and misery.

The Lotus Sutra passages cited by Nichiren in “The Selection of the Time,” which I discussed earlier, accurately describe the reality of the “age of quarrels and disputes.” Evil demons possess the minds of arrogant priests who are hungry for renown and profit and cause them to repudiate the correct teaching and its

votary. In a sense, there is no greater ignorance than this. The workings of fundamental darkness seek to prevent people’s innate enlightened nature from manifesting.

Battling fundamental darkness is tantamount to battling the evil or negativity inherent in human life. Genuine practitioners of the Lotus Sutra do not retreat a single step in this struggle. And by triumphing over the three obstacles and four devils,16 and also the onslaughts of the three powerful enemies, practitioners can eloquently demonstrate the brilliant potential of the fundamental nature of enlightenment or Dharma nature17 that all people possess. This proof upsets others’ erroneous attachments and causes them to question their beliefs.

The struggle against fundamental darkness is a challenge to break through people’s distrust and disrespect for human life and awaken them to the innate dignity of the human being. In this sense, I think we can also view Nichiren’s warning of “great struggles and disputes such as have never been known in the past” as a deliberate attempt to

unsettle people’s erroneous beliefs in order to break through the walls of prejudice and powerlessness in their hearts.

Our humanity must win over the devilish nature inherent in life. This is the struggle of kosen-rufu.

The purpose of Buddhism is to enfold humankind in a warm current of compassion. Mr. Toda said: “When you practice Buddhism in a way that truly matches the time, you can naturally take compassionate action.” Our efforts to treasure each person are building a compassionate society of respect for all.

Mr. Toda also said that as ordinary people we need courage to continue taking compassionate action. This, he declared, is none other than the courage to help others, the courage to improve ourselves, the courage to challenge our human revolution and the courage to work for kosen-rufu. Now, in the 21st century, will the ideas of respect for the sanctity of life and human dignity come to be widely established through a spiritual solidarity of conscientious

people across the globe? Or will we see a prevalence of ideas that denigrate the sanctity of life and human dignity, resulting from a causal chain of mistrust and hatred generated by fundamental darkness or ignorance? I believe it is deeply meaningful that, as humanity now stands at this crossroads, our Soka alliance is steadily spreading throughout the world with a commitment to foster and promote positive human values. People’s hearts are swayed by the currents of the times.

Read aloud · Create the right timeWithout positive action, there is a danger of losing all direction. A strong determination to move the times toward solid good is the driving force that propels us to create the right time. The “time” is not simply an objective condition; it is carved, in essence, by a firm will. In other words, a sound age is created by the will to fight and win, day after day, steadily and surely, no matter whether anyone is watching our efforts or not. Mr. Toda said: “We should focus our efforts on fostering one new sincere member, and then foster another, and another. This is what creates the time.” In this sense, the right time is not something that we wait for, but something that we ourselves must create. This is what I learned from my mentor during my youthful struggles at his side.

I have always taken action with the conviction that the right time is something we achieve through determination and effort, that it all comes down to personally challenging ourselves. Rather than sitting in quiet contemplation, I have chanted daimoku and taken action—traveling, speaking and writing tirelessly to spread the philosophy of Nichiren Buddhism—opening the doors of people’s hearts and helping them construct an invincible fortress of peace and happiness in their lives. I am also confident that this is what it means to “select the time”—or choose the right time—in terms of our individual Buddhist practice. Nichiren Daishonin, the Buddha of the Latter Day, said: “I, Nichiren, conscious of the age we live in, now wish to give wide propagation to this doctrine [of the Three Great Secret Laws (i.e., Nam-myoho-renge-kyo)]” (“Receiving of the Three Great Secret Laws,” WND-2, 988).

Notes

1. One of the five types of vision—the physical eyes, the heavenly eye, the wisdom eye, the Dharma eye and the Buddha eye. The “eye of the Buddha” here means the sutras that consist of Shakyamuni’s teachings.

2. Great Collection Sutra: A collection of sutras translated into Chinese by Dharmaraksha (385-433) and others. These sutras were compiled into a single sutra, or the Great Collection Sutra, by Seng-chiu of the Sui dynasty in 586. The work predicts how the spread of Buddhism will unfold over the five five-hundred-year periods following Shakyamuni’s death.

3. Amida is a Buddha described in the Pure Land sutras as dwelling in the Pure Land of Perfect Bliss in the west. He is revered by the followers of the Pure Land, or Nembutsu, school.

4. Five five-hundred-year periods: Five consecutive periods following Shakyamuni’s death, during which Buddhism is said to spread, prosper and eventually decline. They are 1) the “age of attaining liberation,” in which many people attain emancipation through practicing the Buddha’s teachings; 2) the “age of meditation,” when meditation is widely practiced; 3) the “age of reading, reciting and listening,” in which the people study and recite the sutras and hear lectures on them as their central practice; 4) the “age of building temples and stupas,” when many temples and stupas are built, but the spirit of seeking the Buddhist teachings declines; and 5) the “age of conflict,” also known as the “age of quarrels and disputes,” when strife occurs among the various rival Buddhist schools and Shakyamuni Buddha’s teachings become obscured and lost. This fifth of these fivehundred-year periods corresponds to the beginning of the Latter Day of the Law.

5. Eight or ten schools: The eight schools are the eight major schools of Buddhism in Japan in the Nara (710-94) and Heian (794—1185) peri-

ods. They are the Dharma Analysis Treasury, Establishment of Truth, Precepts, Dharma Characteristics, Three Treatises, Flower Garland, Tendai and True Word schools. Pure Land (Nembutsu) and Zen came to prominence early in the Kamakura period (1185-1333), and the addition of these schools brought Japan’s major Buddhist schools to ten total. Japanese Buddhist works often refer to them collectively as the ten schools.

6. Three Pure Land sutras: The three basic sutras of the different Pure Land schools in Japan. They are the Buddha Infinite Life Sutra, the Meditation on the Buddha Infinite Life Sutra and the Amida Sutra. Honen (1133-1212), the founder of the Japanese Pure Land school, termed these three the three Pure Land sutras. The three sutras praise the Pure Land of Perfect Bliss, i.e., the Pure Land of Amida Buddha, and exhort people to aspire to rebirth there.

7. Buddhism of sowing: The Buddhism that plants the seeds of Buddhahood, or the cause for attaining Buddhahood, in people’s lives. In the teachings of Nichiren Daishonin,

the Buddhism of sowing indicates Nichiren Buddhism in contrast with that of Shakyamuni, which is called the Buddhism of the harvest. The Buddhism of the harvest is that which can lead to enlightenment only those who received the seeds of Buddhahood by practicing the Buddha’s teaching in previous lifetimes. In contrast, the Buddhism of sowing implants the seed of Buddhahood, or Nam-myohorengekyo, in the lives of those who had no connection with the Buddha’s teaching in their past existences, i.e., the people of the Latter Day of the Law. 8. Many Treasures Buddha: A Buddha depicted in the Lotus Sutra. Many Treasures appears, seated within his treasure tower, in order to lend credence to Shakyamuni’s teachings in the sutra. According to “Treasure Tower,’ the 11th chapter of the Lotus Sutra, Many Treasures Buddha lives in the World of Treasure Purity in the east. While still engaged in bodhisattva practice, he pledges that, even after entering nirvana, he will appear with his treasure tower in order to attest to the validity of the Lotus Sutra, wherever it might be taught.

9. These are gods and kings depicted in Buddhist mythology. Brahma and Shakra are the two principal tutelary gods of Buddhism. The four heavenly kings serve Shakra and protect the four quarters of the world.

10. The Daishonin refers to the passage from “Medicine King,” the 23rd chapter: “After I have passed into extinction, in the last five-hundredyear period you must spread it [this teaching] abroad widely throughout Jambudvipa [the entire world] and never allow it to be cut off, nor must you allow evil devils, the devils’ people, heavenly beings, dragons, yakshas, kumbhanda demons, or others to seize the advantage!” (The Lotus Sutra and Its Opening and Closing Sutras, p. 330.) He also cites the following passages from “Encouraging Devotion,” the 13th chapter: “In that evil age there will be monks,” “Or there will be forestdwelling monks,” and “Evil demons will take possession of others” (LSOC, pp. 232-33).

11. This refers to the passage from “Encouraging Devotion,” the 13th chapter: “Evil demons will take possession of others.” It means that

negative functions will enter people’s lives, causing them to revile and denigrate those who embrace and protect the correct teaching of Buddhism, and to obstruct their Buddhist practice.

12. Three powerful enemies: Three types of arrogant people who persecute those who propagate the Lotus Sutra in the evil age after Shakyamuni Buddha’s death, described in the concluding verse section of “Encouraging Devotion,” the 13th chapter of the Lotus Sutra. The Great Teacher Miao-lo (711-82) of China summarizes them as arrogant lay people, arrogant priests and arrogant false sages.

13. 14. The seven disasters are said to be caused by slander of the correct teaching. The Medicine Master Sutra defines the seven disasters as: 1) pestilence, 2) foreign invasion, 3) internal strife, 4) extraordinary changes in the heavens, 5) solar and lunar eclipses, 6) unseasonable storms and 7) drought.

15. Fundamental darkness or ignorance: The most deeply rooted illusion inherent in life, said to give rise to all other illusions. Fundamental darkness means the inability to see or recognize the truth, particularly the true nature of one’s life. Nichiren Daishonin interprets fundamental darkness as ignorance of the ultimate Law, or ignorance of the fact that one’s life is essentially a manifestation of the Law, which he identifies as Nam-myoho-renge-kyo.

16. Three obstacles and four devils: Various obstacles and hindrances to the practice of Buddhism. The three obstacles are 1) the obstacle of earthly desires, 2) the obstacle of karma and 3) the obstacle of retribution. The four devils are 1) the hindrance of the earthly desires, 2) the hindrance of the five compon-

ents, 3) the hindrance of death and 4) the hindrance of the devil king. 17. Fundamental nature of enlightenment, or Dharma nature: The unchanging nature inherent in all things and phenomena.
